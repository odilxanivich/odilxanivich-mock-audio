## SECTION 3

**Dr Morris:** Come in, both of you. I've read your draft report on food waste in the university canteen. Before we go through it, remind me how you came to choose this topic.

**Daniel:** Originally we wanted to write about energy use in the dormitories. You suggested that, remember?

**Dr Morris:** I did, yes.

**Seo-yeon:** But then we noticed how much food was left on the trays in the canteen every lunchtime. It was our own canteen, so we thought it would be more interesting to measure something we could see ourselves.

**Dr Morris:** Good. A topic you actually observe makes for a stronger project than one taken from a news article. And you chose to weigh the food?

**Daniel:** Yes. We thought about a questionnaire for students, but people don't always tell the truth about how much they throw away. So we weighed the leftovers at the collection point, every day for five days, at lunchtime.

**Dr Morris:** And did the data collection go smoothly?

**Seo-yeon:** Mostly. The canteen staff were really helpful, and the holiday didn't affect us, because we started after it. The only problem was that on Wednesday one of the scales stopped working, so we had to borrow another one from the chemistry lab.

**Dr Morris:** That's worth mentioning in the method, because different scales can give slightly different readings. Now, five days. That's a small sample.

**Daniel:** We know. We were worried it was too small.

**Dr Morris:** For a full study, yes. For a small project like this, it's perfectly acceptable, as long as you say clearly that it's a pilot study and you don't make big claims. *[pause]*

**Narrator:** Before you hear the rest of the conversation, you have some time to look at Questions 26 to 30. *[pause 20 seconds]*

**Seo-yeon:** OK, we'll say that.

**Dr Morris:** Let's go through the report section by section. Start with the introduction. It's well written, but I couldn't find the aim of your project. You describe the background for two pages, but you never say what you're trying to find out.

**Daniel:** So we need a clear aim.

**Dr Morris:** Exactly. The literature review next. You've used some useful articles, but most of them are from before 2010. There's a lot of more recent research, especially from Korea and Japan, so look for something newer.

**Seo-yeon:** We'll do that.

**Dr Morris:** The methodology section is very clear. I'd use it as an example for other groups, particularly the diagram of the collection point. Keep it as it is.

**Daniel:** Great.

**Dr Morris:** The results section has good tables, but there's one mistake. In the second chart, the labels on the vertical axis say kilograms, but the numbers are actually in grams. That makes the waste look a thousand times bigger. Please check it.

**Seo-yeon:** Oh no. We'll fix that.

**Dr Morris:** Finally, the conclusion. It's too long. You repeat most of the results. Half a page is enough.

**Daniel:** OK.

**Dr Morris:** One more thing, the presentation next week. You have ten minutes. Most groups run over, so practise the timing a few times with a stopwatch, rather than adding more slides.

**Seo-yeon:** Should we include a demonstration of how we weighed the food?

**Dr Morris:** That's not necessary. A photo is enough. Spend the time on practice.

**Daniel:** Thank you, Dr Morris.

## SECTION 4

**Narrator:** Section 4. You will hear a lecture about traditional Korean houses. You now have thirty seconds to look at Questions 31 to 40. *[pause 30 seconds]*

**Professor Kang:** Good afternoon. Today's lecture is about the traditional Korean house, the hanok, and what has happened to it over the last hundred years.

Let's begin with the position. A traditional hanok was never built just anywhere. The ideal site had a mountain behind it and a river or stream in front of it, and the house faced south. So when we talk about the position of a hanok, the first thing to remember is the mountain at the back. It protected the house from the cold winter winds.

Now, the materials. People used what was available locally. The roof is covered with curved tiles, and these tiles are made of clay, fired in a kiln. Some poorer families used straw instead, that is, thatch, but tiles were a sign of status. The walls were a wooden frame filled with a mixture of clay and straw. The doors and windows are covered with traditional paper, called hanji. Many people think hanji is made from rice, but it's actually made from the bark of the mulberry tree. It's strong, it lets light through, and it allows the house to breathe.

Next, heating. The famous system is ondol, which means "warm stone". A fire in the kitchen heats the air and smoke, which travel through channels under the floor. The floor itself is made from thin stone slabs, which absorb the heat and release it slowly, so a room can stay warm for hours after the fire has gone out. This was a very efficient system, using little fuel.

And in summer? Hanok designers solved the problem of heat with a large, open room with a wooden floor, called the maru. That's M-A-R-U. It's raised above the ground and open to the garden, so the air moves freely through it.

So why did these houses almost disappear? After the 1960s, Korea changed very quickly. The population of the cities grew, and land became expensive. Developers replaced hanok with tall apartment blocks, which could house many more families. Concrete was cheaper than timber, too. By the 1990s, only a few traditional neighbourhoods were left.

Fortunately, attitudes have changed. Today the government gives financial support to owners who restore their old houses. They don't give money for demolition. And the modern hanok is not a museum piece. Architects have kept the traditional look, but they have added new features. Modern hanok are built with insulation inside the walls and the floors, so that the heat doesn't escape.

Tourism has created new opportunities. A large number of hanok in Seoul and Jeonju are now run as guesthouses, where visitors can sleep on the floor, just as people did centuries ago. However, this has brought problems. Residents of areas like Bukchon complain about the noise, of course, but what worries them most is the loss of privacy, because tourists photograph into private gardens. Some districts have now introduced limits on visiting hours.

To conclude, the hanok shows how a traditional design can adapt to modern life, but only if the needs of the people who actually live there are respected. Thank you.

**Narrator:** That is the end of Section 4. You now have two minutes to check your answers.
